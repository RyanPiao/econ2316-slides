From One Price to Many

Ch 11 — single price

  • One price for all buyers: \(MR = MC\)
  • Markup \(L = (P-MC)/P = -1/\varepsilon_D\)
  • Captures only the rectangle below \(P_M\)
  • Leaves consumer surplus and deadweight loss on the table

Ch 12 — many prices

  • Different prices to different buyers
  • Six tools: 1st / 2nd / 3rd-degree, two-part tariffs, bundling, platforms
  • Converts that left-behind CS into producer surplus
  • DWL falls when output rises — to zero under perfect PD. When output stays put, total surplus can fall

One assumption relaxes. Chapter 11 forced the monopolist to post a single price. Drop that — let the firm separate buyers it can tell apart, or get them to sort themselves — and the surplus a uniform price couldn't reach becomes capturable. The common goal of all six tools: convert consumer surplus into producer surplus. The welfare effect on consumers is ambiguous; the effect on the firm's profit never is.

1 · Knows Each Buyer Before the Sale — First-Degree

Pigou’s three degrees (1920), ordered by what the firm knows about its buyers · 1 of 3

First-degree (perfect)

The firm knows each buyer’s whole demand curve before the sale and charges each one their exact reservation price. It captures all consumer surplus, and output equals the competitive level.

Needs: know every buyer’s willingness to pay.

The car lot: the salesperson sizes up each buyer — budget, urgency, trade-in — and negotiates toward your personal maximum. No two buyers on the lot pay the same price for the same car.

2 · Learns Demand Only After the Sale — Second-Degree

Pigou’s three degrees (1920), ordered by what the firm knows about its buyers · 2 of 3

Second-degree (menu)

The firm cannot tell buyers apart before the sale. It offers a menu — versions, bundles, block prices, two-part tariffs — and buyers self-select. The version you pick reveals your type.

Needs: know the mix of buyer types, not who is who.

Spotify versioning: Individual (\$12.99), Duo (\$18.99, 2 accounts), Family (\$21.99, up to 6). Households sort themselves by size, and the cheaper tiers are capped (one account, same address) so bigger households pay more.

3 · Knows Only the Group — Third-Degree

Pigou’s three degrees (1920), ordered by what the firm knows about its buyers · 3 of 3

Third-degree (group)

Less detail: the firm sees only a label — student, location, weekend — and treats everyone in a group as sharing one demand. Then it applies the Ch 11 rule group by group: \(L_i = -1/\varepsilon_{D,i}\).

Needs: observe a label that tracks willingness to pay.

Student discounts: UNiDAYS unlocks deals (HP, Microsoft 365) only with a verified student ID. Every verified student gets the same price: the firm cannot tell one student from another.

On any day in early 2026, Spotify offered five US prices for the same music library — Student, Individual, Duo, Family, and a standalone Audiobooks tier. Same firm, same songs, five separate price decisions.

The Basics of Pricing Strategy

Which pricing tool a firm can use ultimately depends on the information it has about its customers.

1 · Knows demand before the sale

  • Direct PD — price by observable customer traits.
  • Knows each buyer's whole demand curve → first-degree (perfect).
  • Less detail → segment by group → third-degree.

2 · Learns demand only after the sale

  • Indirect / second-degree — offer a menu, buyers self-select.
  • Bundle products into a package.
  • Block pricing & two-part tariffs.

3 · Customers share the same demand

  • Still profitable: block pricing and two-part tariffs capture extra surplus.

Pigou's Three Degrees — Ordered by What the Firm Must Know

Arthur Cecil Pigou, The Economics of Welfare (1920). Still the working taxonomy a century later.

First-degree (perfect)

Charge each buyer their exact reservation price. Captures all CS; output equals competitive. Needs: know every buyer's WTP.

Second-degree (menu)

Offer a menu; buyers self-select. The firm can't see types but designs choices so types reveal themselves. Needs: know the distribution.

Third-degree (group)

Segment by an observable label (student, weekend) and apply the Ch 11 rule per group: \(L_i = -1/\varepsilon_{D,i}\). Needs: observe a label that correlates with WTP.

Descending informational demand: know each buyer → know the distribution → observe a group label. Each degree extracts less than the one above but is more achievable. Real firms mix all three — Spotify uses group labels (Student), a menu (Audiobooks vs Premium), and edges toward personalization.

The Catch: No Arbitrage

Every PD scheme must block arbitrage — resale from the low-price segment to the high-price one. If a student bought an HP laptop at the UNiDAYS price and resold it to a non-student, the student price would collapse.

Heads Up 12.1 — PD needs market power and no arbitrage

Not every price difference is price discrimination. PD requires (a) market power (so \(P > MC\) is sustainable) and (b) no arbitrage between segments.

Two competitive sellers at different prices is price dispersion (search costs), not PD. And a monopolist who cannot stop resale is forced back to a single price — however different its buyers' WTPs.

Without an anti-arbitrage mechanism, PD collapses into a single price. The verification technology — ID checks, contracts, DRM, geography — is what makes the whole chapter possible.

First-Degree (Perfect) PD

The upper bound: capture every dollar, restore efficiency — \(Q_{PD} = Q_C\), \(CS = 0\)

First-Degree — Each Buyer’s Exact Willingness to Pay

The firm captures every dollar of consumer surplus. Nearly impossible — but the data economy is closing the gap.

What it takes — all three, or it collapses

1. Market power. Demand must slope down. A price-taker has no price to discriminate with.

2. No arbitrage. The low-price buyer must not be able to resell to the high-price one (§12.1).

3. Know each buyer's WTP. The hard one — and the reason first-degree stays a bound.

It’s direct PD — with complete information about customers, the firm charges each buyer exactly their willingness to pay. So what happens to consumer surplus? Under perfect PD it is driven to zero: every dollar of surplus is converted into producer surplus.

What If the Firm Could Read Every Mind?

A monopolist who knows each buyer's exact reservation sells one unit to everyone whose WTP exceeds \(MC\), charging each their reservation:

Output expands to the competitive level \(Q_{PD} = Q_C\) — no deadweight loss. But the firm takes every dollar below demand and above \(MC\): \(CS = 0\), and producer surplus equals total surplus.

Perfect PD is the only form of market power that is allocatively efficient — the efficiency loss of monopoly comes from the single price, not from the markup itself. It is also the hardest to achieve: it needs each buyer's WTP.

The trap — monopoly is not the inefficiency

Most students answer that monopoly causes deadweight loss. It doesn't. The single price does.

Remove the single price and the inefficiency vanishes while the market power remains: output returns to \(Q_C\), \(DWL = 0\), and the entire surplus simply changes hands. Efficient and maximally extractive at the same time.

The Whole Market: One Price → Perfect PD

Demand \(P = 100 - Q\); \(MC = 20\). With \(k\) optimally-set price blocks, output is \(Q_k = \tfrac{k}{k+1}\,Q_C\) and profit is \(\Pi_k = \tfrac{k}{k+1}(3200)\). At \(k = 1\) this is the uniform monopoly (\(Q = 40\), \(\pi = \$1{,}600\), \(DWL = \$800\)); as \(k\) grows the blue captured surplus rises toward \$3,200 and the red DWL shrinks to zero. This is the earbud market of Walkthrough 12.1, next.

Walkthrough 12.1 — A Price Set From Your Personal Data

Background. Since November 2025, New York has enforced a label on any price set from your data: “THIS PRICE WAS SET BY AN ALGORITHM USING YOUR PERSONAL DATA.” Suppose an online earbud seller’s algorithm really knew each shopper’s maximum. Daily demand is \(P^d(Q) = 100 - Q\) (\(Q\) = shoppers, \$ per pair), and each pair costs \(MC = \$20\).

Problem. Compare (a) competition, (b) one posted price, (c) the algorithm charging each shopper their own maximum. Tally CS, PS and DWL in each.

Set up & solve

(a) Competition \(P=MC\): \(Q_C = 80\), \(P_C = \$20\). \(CS = \tfrac12(80)(80) = \$3{,}200\), \(PS = 0\), \(DWL = 0\).

(b) One posted price \(MR = 100 - 2Q = 20\): \(Q_M = 40\), \(P_M = \$60\). \(\pi = (40)(40) = \$1{,}600\), \(CS = \$800\), \(DWL = \$800\).

Perfect PD & check

(c) A price per shopper (perfect PD): every shopper who values a pair above \$20 buys, so \(Q_{PD} = 80\). Profit is the area under demand above \(MC\):

\(\pi_{PD} = \tfrac12 \times 80 \times 80 = \$3{,}200\) (the triangle between demand and \(MC\))

\(CS_{PD} = 0\), \(DWL = 0\). Profit equals competitive total surplus: the seller takes everything.

Takeaway. Seller/shoppers: \(0/\$3{,}200\) (competition) → \(\$1{,}600/\$800\) (one price) → \(\$3{,}200/0\) (perfect PD). Perfect PD is efficient and takes every dollar of surplus, which is why New York wants it labeled. Real sellers fall short for lack of information: on Instacart, different shoppers saw five prices for the same dozen eggs, \$3.99–\$4.79 (Consumer Reports, Dec 2025; Instacart calls them random tests).

Second-Degree — Build a Menu, Let Buyers Sort Themselves

The firm can’t tell who you are — so it designs options and lets you reveal your type by choosing.

Versioning. Spotify: Individual $12.99 → Duo $18.99 → Family $21.99, same catalog, tiers gated by account count.

Quantity discounts. Costco 50-bag box at $0.45/bag vs $0.50 at the corner store.

Two-part tariffs. Gym: $300/yr + $10/class. Buffet: $20 in, $0/plate.

Bundling. $15 CD (12 songs) → iTunes $0.99 unbundled → Spotify re-bundles 100M songs at $12.99.

Netflix tiers: $6.99 ad-supported vs $22.99 premium — a $16 gap for the same library. Cinephiles self-select up; budget households (higher elasticity) self-select into ads. ~70M ad-tier users by 2025.

It’s indirect PD — used when the firm has market power and can prevent resale, customers have different demand curves, but the firm can’t identify a buyer’s type before purchase. Its tool: post a menu of pricing options and let customers self-select.

Two Rules Every Menu Must Pass

The firm can’t see your type, so it can’t assign you a tier. It can only set prices so that each type chooses the tier built for it. Every type has to pass two checks:

IR · rule 1

Individual rationality

“Is my tier worth buying at all?”

value of my tier − its price ≥ 0

Spotify: is Duo (2 accounts, \$18.99 a month) worth it to a couple? If not, they walk away and the firm earns nothing from them.

IC · rule 2

Incentive compatibility

“Would I rather have the tier meant for someone else?”

surplus on my tier ≥ surplus on any other tier

Spotify: would a household of five rather squeeze onto Duo than pay for Family (up to 6 accounts)? If yes, Family never sells.

Which rule pinches whom? For the low type, IR is tight: the firm charges up to its full value. For the high type, IC is tight: it must be left enough surplus not to buy down. That surplus is its information rent.

Versioning — Buyers Self-Select

Versioning: the firm offers different versions of one product, each designed for a different type of buyer. Each buyer picks the version best for them, and the pick reveals their type.

Spotify: Individual (\$12.99), Duo (\$18.99, 2 accounts), Family (\$21.99, up to 6). Households sort themselves by size; the cheaper tiers are capped (one account, same address) so bigger households pay more.

No cost difference needed: the versions can cost the same to make; the less elastic group (big households) pays the higher markup.

Versioning — Buyers Self-Select

Offer a menu of versions and let each buyer voluntarily pick the one best for them — revealing their type. The firm prices by the version chosen, not by who you are. To make buyers split, firms widen the gap between tiers — sometimes intentionally limiting the cheaper one (the “damaged-goods” idea: stripped Basic Economy, a deliberately crippled chip).

Spotify is textbook versioning: Individual ($12.99), Duo ($18.99 / 2 accounts), Family ($21.99 / up to 6 accounts). Households self-select by size, and the cheaper tiers are deliberately capped (one account, same-address rules) so higher-value households pay more.

Self-Sorting Captures the Entire Demand Curve

Highly price-inelastic. Cinephiles & high-income households want 4K HDR — they pay $22.99+/mo and refuse to watch ads.
Highly price-elastic. Budget households & students — captured by the $6.99 ad-supported tier (~70M monthly active users by 2024).

To fund $17B+/yr of content like Stranger Things, Netflix uses the identical core product to monetize the bottom of the curve without sacrificing the margins at the top.

The “Stripped” Economy — Damaged Goods

The flip side of versioning: a firm will deliberately make the cheap version worse — spending nothing, or even money, to degrade it — so high-WTP buyers refuse to trade down and pay for the premium tier.

Then. 1840s railways ran roofless third-class cars. Dupuit (1849): “not to hurt the poor, but to frighten the rich.”

Now. Ever-tighter “stripped” economy seats and Basic Economy keep the cheap tier just painful enough to upsell.

§12.4 — the central tool

Third-Degree (Group) PD

The Ch 11 inverse-elasticity rule, applied per segment: \(L_i = -\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon_{D,i}}\)

Third-Degree PD — Price by Observable Group

Spotify: Individual \$12.99, Student \$6.99. The .edu check is the fence that stops resale.

The firm sees a label (student, age, location, time) and charges the group, not the person.

It works when

1. the firm has market power and can stop resale
2. the groups differ in price sensitivity
3. the firm can tell the groups apart before the sale

The rule: the less elastic group pays more, the more elastic group pays less. Students are price-sensitive, so they get \$6.99.

Third-degree: the seller sees your group and assigns the price.

Second-degree: the seller posts a menu and you pick.

Third-Degree — Different Price to an Observable Group

Spotify. \$12.99 Individual vs \$6.99 Student. Students have higher elasticity; a .edu check prevents arbitrage. Charge them full price and many leave.

The logic is always elasticity: charge the inelastic group more, the elastic group less. Segment by an observable trait — age, student status, location, timing — and stop resale.

Third-degree. The seller observes your group and assigns the price. You cannot opt in.

Second-degree. The seller posts a menu and you pick. Your choice reveals your type.

Same elasticity logic driving both — the difference is who does the sorting. This is the most-tested distinction in the chapter.

Third-Degree — the Student Discount

A segment is only worth defining if you can verify it. The .edu check is doing the real work here — it is the arbitrage fence. No fence, no segment: one resold login and the \$6.99 tier becomes everyone’s price.

Third-Degree — Student Platforms (UNiDAYS)

Platforms like UNiDAYS gate deals (HP, Microsoft 365) behind a verified student ID. The long-run twist: once students learn these tools, they keep using them at work — becoming more inelastic later, when they'll pay full price.

Third-Degree — Movie Tickets and Airline Fares

Movie tickets · by age

  • Trait: age. Children and seniors pay less than adults.
  • Why: they are more elastic; the discount fills empty seats at MC ≈ 0.
  • Fence: the ticket is checked at the door.

Airline fares · by trip type

  • Trait: leisure (elastic) vs business (inelastic).
  • Why: fares rise near departure (business books late); early and off-peak = leisure.
  • Fence: the ticket must be used by the purchaser.

Same recipe both times: a trait the firm can see that tracks elasticity, plus a fence that stops resale.

Third-Degree — Airline Fares

Airlines charge varying fares to leisure (elastic) vs business (inelastic) travelers.

  • Prices rise as the departure date nears (late buyers = business).
  • Lower fares on non-peak days & with advance booking (leisure).
  • The ticket must be used by the purchaser — arbitrage blocked.

How Much Should Each Segment Pay?

Where the rule comes from

Segments can’t resell to each other, so profit is just a sum of separate markets:

\(\pi = \sum_i (P_i - MC)\,Q_i(P_i)\)

Each segment gets its own first-order condition:

\(Q_i + (P_i - MC)\,Q_i' = 0\)

which rearranges to

\(L_i = \dfrac{P_i - MC}{P_i} = -\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon_{D,i}}\)

Chapter 11’s Lerner rule, run once per segment.

Starbucks (MC \$0.90). Markup first, then price: \(P = \dfrac{MC}{1 - L}\)

Commuters \(\varepsilon_D = -1.327\): \(L = 0.7534 \Rightarrow P = \tfrac{0.90}{0.2466} = \$3.65\)

Residents \(\varepsilon_D = -1.439\): \(L = 0.6949 \Rightarrow P = \tfrac{0.90}{0.3051} = \$2.95\)

The less elastic segment pays more. Same coffee, same MC: commuters pay about 24% more because their demand is less sensitive to price. The same \$3.65 and \$2.95 as the MR = MC solve.

Calculus Corner 12.1 — Inverse-Elasticity Rule, Per Segment

With \(K\) non-arbitrageable segments and constant \(MC\), maximize

\[ \pi = \sum_i (P_i - MC)\,Q_i(P_i) \]

Each segment's FOC is independent of the others:

\[ Q_i + (P_i - MC)\,Q_i' = 0 \]

Multiply by \(P_i/Q_i\) and rearrange:

\[ L_i = \frac{P_i - MC}{P_i} = -\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{D,i}} \]

Equivalently \(P_i = \dfrac{MC}{1 + 1/\varepsilon_{D,i}}\). The rule from Ch 11 simply fires \(K\) times in parallel — once per segment.

The more-elastic segment gets the smaller markup. Students pay less because their demand is more elastic; business travelers pay more because theirs is not.

The only difference from Ch 11: Ch 11 had one segment, Ch 12 has \(K\). Same engine, run per group — segments don't interact because there's no arbitrage between them.

Heads Up 12.3 — PD welfare is ambiguous

PD does not always reduce welfare. If PD lets the firm serve a market it would otherwise abandon, total surplus rises (output expands). If it only redistributes CS→PS, surplus is unchanged. If it cuts output (rare), surplus falls.

Evidence is mixed: Williams (2022, airlines) finds dynamic pricing raises welfare via output expansion; Crawford-Yurukoglu (2012, cable) finds CS falls ~5%. The welfare question is empirical, not theoretical.

The “Pink Tax”: Price Discrimination or a Cost Difference?

What is the “pink tax”? Not a tax at all: it is the name for paying more for a product or service marketed to women than for a nearly identical one marketed to men, such as a pink razor, a women’s shampoo, or a haircut or dry cleaning. The term spread after New York City’s 2015 study From Cradle to Cane, which compared about 800 products with clear women’s and men’s versions. The economic question is why the price differs.

The observation. Products aimed at women often cost more. The 2015 NYC audit found an average gap of 7% across 35 categories (13% in personal care).

Story 1: price discrimination. Women switch brands less (less elastic), so firms charge a higher markup.

Story 2: cost difference. Different ingredients and packaging cost more to make.

The evidence (goods): compare the same brand with the same main ingredients and the gap almost disappears; women’s razors are even cheaper (Moshary, Tuchman & Bhatia, Marketing Science 2023). So for goods it is mostly cost, not PD. For services like haircuts, it is still unsettled.

The law uses the same test. New York (2020) and California (in force 2023) ban gender-based prices for substantially similar goods; a gap is allowed only where cost really differs.

Cage-Free Eggs — Discrimination or Cost?

Same test as the pink tax: is the price gap bigger than the cost gap?

1 · The cost gap. More space and labor per hen: cage-free eggs cost about 25% more to produce (UC Davis study).

2 · The price gap. In grocery scanner data the cage-free premium averaged about 57%, and about 42% of that came from egg color (brown), not the hens. Chang, Lusk & Norwood (2010).

Price gap > cost gap: part of the premium looks like versioning: a “boutique” label lets stores charge less price-sensitive shoppers more (Lusk names exactly this possibility, along with higher shelf costs).

3 · Then the laws. California (Prop 12) and Massachusetts (Question 3) allow only cage-free eggs from 2022. With every egg cage-free there is no menu left to sort shoppers: the higher price is now a cost difference everyone pays, about 72¢ a dozen in California in early 2022 (Lusk’s estimate).

4 · Boston, 2016: the cheapest cage-free dozen cost 20¢ more at a Back Bay Trader Joe’s, and \$1.10 more at a Dorchester Star Market (Boston Globe). Sound familiar?

Both. Before the laws, cost plus a discrimination markup on a label. After the laws, mostly cost. The test is the same as for the pink tax: compare like with like.

The Pink Tax: Price Discrimination — or the Evidence?

Hypothesis 1 — Price Discrimination. Firms charge women higher markups because their demand is less price-elastic (lower willingness to switch brands).

Hypothesis 2 — Cost Difference. Recent studies on goods (e.g. shampoo) challenge the PD story:

  • Moshary, Tuchman & Bhatia (Marketing Science, 2023): hold brand and leading ingredients fixed and the gap essentially disappears — women’s unit prices come out 0.05% lower, and cheaper in 3 of 5 categories. Women’s deodorant does carry a premium; women’s razors are cheaper — the poster child of the pink tax runs the wrong way.
  • Barnes & Brounstein: estimated demand is slightly more elastic for women — under the inverse-elasticity rule that pushes their price down, the opposite of what H1 needs.

Conclusion (goods): the evidence points to different production costs (pricier ingredients/packaging), not gender-based price discrimination.

Caveat: for services like haircuts the cause is still unsettled — could be higher cost or discrimination.

The law ended up adopting the same test. California allows a price gap only where cost or production time genuinely differs, and defines “substantially similar” as same brand, same materials, similar use and function — which is the economists’ control. Statute and study converged on one identification problem.

A visible price gap is not proof of discrimination — you need evidence on costs and elasticities.

Application: Price Discrimination in the European Car Market

The strategy — segment by country. Automakers (e.g. VW) sell the same car at different prices across European countries, charging higher markups where local demand is less elastic.

Preventing resale (arbitrage):

  • manuals printed only in the local language;
  • servicing restricted to the country of purchase;
  • dealers punished for selling across borders.

The evidence (Goldberg & Verboven): the same VW Golf varies sharply. In 2015, Portugal > Germany > Greece — a Golf was ~20% more expensive in Germany than Greece, but ~20% cheaper than in Portugal.

Some of the gap is taxes — but much is third-degree price discrimination: higher prices where demand is less elastic (Portugal), lower where it's more elastic (Greece), with arbitrage actively blocked.

  • Market power: one subway system.
  • No resale: the cap counts taps on one card or phone.
  • Two blocks: \$3.00 a ride, then \$0.

Two-Part Tariff

A two-part tariff is a pricing strategy in which the payment has two components: a fixed fee \(F\) plus a per-unit price \(p\)  —  the customer pays \(F + p \times Q\).

The fixed fee gets you in the door; the per-unit price is charged on use:

Video game systems
fixed fee = console · per-unit = each game
Zipcar
fixed fee = annual membership · per-unit = hourly rental
Costco
fixed fee = annual membership · per-unit = each item
Amazon Prime
fixed fee = yearly charge · per-unit = any purchase

The annual fee captures consumer surplus up front; the per-class price = marginal cost, so members keep using the gym efficiently instead of being priced out.

You are already inside one. Marino comes bundled into the student fee — that is Part 1 — while personal training and some specialty programs are charged on top. Part 2.

Commercial gyms offer the same two dials as a choice: a day pass (\(F=0\)) or a monthly membership (\(p=0\)). Same sorting as the car wash next slide.

Two-Part Tariff in Steps: Why Costco Charges \$65

One member’s demand, stylized: \(P = 23 - 1.3Q\); Costco’s cost \(MC = \$10\). Set the two parts in order:

Step 1 · the shelf price. Set \(p = MC = \$10\). The member buys the efficient amount, \(Q = 10\), where \(D = MC\).

Pause · how big can the pie get? At \(p = MC\) every unit worth more than it costs gets bought, so the whole triangle between \(D\) and \(MC\) is surplus: the biggest pie possible. Any shelf price above \$10 shrinks it.

Step 2 · the membership. Charge the whole triangle as the fee: ½ × 10 × \$13 = \$65, Costco’s Gold Star fee.

Step 3 · compare one price. \(MR = MC\): \(Q = 5\) at \$16.50. Profit is only B = \$32.50; A = \$16.25 stays with the member, C = \$16.25 is lost.

Step 4 · the real Costco. FY2025: membership fees \$5.3B, about half of operating income; about 90% of members renew. The shelf runs close to cost.

Price use at MC, charge the triangle at the door. The member buys the efficient amount, there is no DWL, and the firm collects all \$65 (vs \$32.50 at one price). With \(MC \approx 0\) (gym, buffet, Disneyland), the fee is the whole area under demand.

Calculus Corner 12.2 — The Two-Part Tariff Lagrangian

Identical consumers, demand \(q(p)\), and \(CS(p)\) = the area under demand above \(p\). Raising \(p\) by \$1 costs each buyer about \(q\) dollars of surplus, so \(\partial CS/\partial p = -q\). Maximize profit per consumer subject to participation:

\[ \mathcal{L} = F + (p - MC)\,q(p) + \lambda\,[CS(p) - F] \]

FOC in \(F\): \(1 - \lambda = 0 \Rightarrow \lambda = 1\) — participation binds. FOC in \(p\):

\[ q + (p - MC)\,q' - q = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; (p - MC)\,q' = 0 \]

Since \(q' < 0\), the only solution is \(p = MC\).

\( p^{\ast} = MC, \qquad F^{\ast} = CS(MC) \)

Profit per consumer \(= CS(MC)\) — the entire competitive surplus. Output equals the competitive level, so there's no DWL; the entry fee converts what would have been CS into a lump-sum extraction.

This is why Disney charges high gate admission, Costco high membership, AWS large up-front commitments: \(F\) is the surplus-capture instrument; pricing usage near MC maximizes the surplus there is to capture.

Application 12.2 — Disneyland: From One Fee to Two Prices

1 · Before 2021: one dial. Pay at the gate, then every ride is free: \(p = 0\). The gate fee collects the whole triangle.

2 · Why \(p = 0\)? One more ride costs Disney about nothing, so \(p = MC \approx 0\) and the fee does the work: the rule from the last slide.

3 · 2021: a second dial. Lightning Lane lets you pay to skip the line, ride by ride or for the day. The ride itself is still free with admission; what you buy is time.

4 · Why now? Crowds. A short wait is scarce, so it has a real cost. Price it, and the guests who value their time most pay for it.

Two-part tariff, \(F + p\): the gate is \(F\); the per-ride price now sits on the scarce extra, the wait, not the ride. 2026: one-day tickets about \$104–\$224 by date; Lightning Lane Multi Pass about \$35 a day.

§12.6

Bundling

When valuations are negatively correlated, the package beats the parts

Bundling

Bundling — a pricing strategy in which a firm sells two or more products together at a single price.

When to use it

1. The firm has market power and can prevent resale.

2. Consumers' demand for the second product is negatively correlated with their demand for the first.

Everyday example: Disney+, Hulu and HBO Max sold together for \$21.99 a month (with ads). Bought one at a time, Disney+ is \$12.49 and HBO Max is \$10.99: just two of the three already cost more than the whole bundle.

Theory and Data 12.2 — Cable TV, and the Cousin of Bundling: Tying

Crawford-Yurukoglu (2012, AER)

Across 33 metro markets, bundling channels into tiers (vs selling each channel separately) reduces consumer surplus ~5% on average — but total-welfare effects range \(-1.7\%\) to \(+6.0\%\), because tiering keeps niche networks alive that wouldn't survive standalone.

Tying — procompetitive or not?

Tying: sell A only if the buyer also takes B. Microsoft 1998 (IE + Windows); EU fined Apple €1.8B (Mar 2024) for App Store anti-steering; Apple-Epic ruling (Apr 2025).

Two theories: Adams-Yellen (procompetitive surplus capture) vs leverage (use dominance in A to foreclose B). Modern antitrust weighs both empirically.

Antitrust Case — US v. Microsoft (1998–2001)

Tying on trial: Windows shipped with Internet Explorer built in, and PC makers were not allowed to remove its icon.

Netscape Navigator, and Bill Gates’s videotaped deposition, 27 Aug 1998.

The monopoly (A): Windows ran on more than 95% of Intel-compatible PCs. The rival (B): Netscape had about 80% of browsers in 1996.

The tie: IE free and built into every copy of Windows. By 2002, IE had about 95% of browsers.

The courts: 1998 DOJ sues → 2000 illegal monopoly, breakup ordered → 2001 appeal: monopoly ruling stands, breakup reversed, tying sent back under the rule of reason → Nov 2001 settlement.

Why tie a free browser? A browser that could run apps on any operating system would have weakened the reason to buy Windows. Tying B protected the monopoly in A: the foreclosure theory, not Adams–Yellen.

Same question, 2024–25. Judge Mehta found Google illegally kept its search monopoly with exclusive default deals (Aug 2024). Remedies (Sep 2025): no forced Chrome sale, exclusive deals banned, some search data shared with rivals. On appeal.

Antitrust Case — Keeping the Market Contestable

A contestable market: entry and exit are cheap, so even a monopolist must act as if a rival could arrive tomorrow. Microsoft’s own economist, Richard Schmalensee, testified in 1999 that much of its competition could not even be named yet: in 1994 no one knew Netscape, Java or Linux would become threats.

1 · What Microsoft had to do (settlement, 2002–2011): no breakup, no US fine, but rules:

  • PC makers may remove IE and ship a rival browser as the default.
  • No retaliation, no exclusive deals.
  • Share the interfaces rival software needs.

2 · Why these rules? Each one lowers a barrier to entry: a way to reach users, to work with Windows, to sign PC makers. The goal was not a lower price; it was keeping the market contestable.

3 · Europe went further: €497M (2004); a browser choice screen from 2010; €561M (2013) when it quietly lapsed.

4 · The result: Firefox (2004), then Chrome (2008); Chrome passed IE in 2012. Statista / StatCounter.

Keep entry open, and the threat of entry does the work. It worked here because a browser is cheap to build and free to download. The Google judge made the same choice in 2025: conduct rules, no forced sale of Chrome.

§12.7

Network Goods & Two-Sided Platforms

Cross-side network effects: the price structure matters, not just the level

What Is a Network Good? — Value From Others

A network good is one whose usefulness depends on the existence of a physical, virtual, or social network — its value comes from the other people using it.

Three ways people get connected

  Physical — wires, towers, rails. Your phone is useless without cell towers.

  Virtual — the same platform or format. A PDF opens anywhere because everyone uses PDF.

  Social — the people you know. A group chat is worth joining only if your friends are in it.

  The newest case: AI chatbots and agents

Meta AI was built into WhatsApp, Instagram, Messenger and Facebook — and reached 1 billion monthly users (May 2025).

Muse, Meta’s AI agent (Sept 2026): message it in WhatsApp and it shops, books travel and fills in forms. 10 million+ people set one up in about six weeks.

Meta did not build a new network. It plugged the AI into one that 3.64 billion people already use every day (Sept 2026) — physical data centers, a virtual app platform, a social graph.

Ch 11 asked why this creates market power. §12.7 asks the harder question: once you have a network good, how do you price it — and to whom?

Network Effects — Why Size Matters for ChatGPT

  ChatGPT: 800 million weekly users (Oct 2025) → 900 million (Feb 2026). Why does size help?

1 · Same-side (direct): your friends. Group chats put up to 20 people in one ChatGPT thread (Nov 2025). Weak, though: a chatbot still works when you use it alone.

2 · A data loop. More chats and thumbs-ups help train the next model for everyone. It runs through OpenAI, not between users, and it fades as data piles up.

3 · Cross-side (indirect): app makers. Apps from Spotify, Zillow and Canva run inside ChatGPT (Oct 2025); any developer could submit one from Dec 2025. More users draw more apps, and more apps draw more users.

Effects 1 and 2 are about one group: one population, one price. Effect 3 adds a second group, and that makes ChatGPT a two-sided platform: it picks a price for each side (G 77–79). Either way, growth feeds on itself.

The External Benefit — When You Join ChatGPT

Private benefit (DP): what you get — your own answers. The market stops where DP meets S, at QP.

Social benefit (DS): your chats and thumbs-ups help train answers for everyone; your group chats bring friends in. You ignore that part when you decide.

The efficient network is QS. There the provider needs PS, but the last user pays only what DP says. The brace is the gap no private price collects — so adoption is too low.

Each new user shifts both curves out again (DP1, DS1). Growth feeds on itself.

Why ChatGPT has a free tier. An owner can collect the gap later, so it prices low to buy users: about 50 million paying subscribers out of 900 million weekly users (Feb 2026). Underpricing is investment in its own demand curve. In reverse, each user who leaves hurts those who stay.

Why the Leader Pulls Away — Metcalfe’s Law

Metcalfe’s Law. \(n\) users make \(\dfrac{n(n-1)}{2}\) possible links, about \(\dfrac{n^2}{2}\). Double the users from 10 to 20: links go from 45 to 190, more than four times.

Why it matters for pricing. The \(n\)th user adds \(n-1\) links: the 5th adds 4, the 20th adds 19. Each user is worth more than the last — so demand can bend the wrong way, and the bigger network pulls away.

The critique (Odlyzko & Tilly). You do not talk to everyone; most links are worth little. Value is nearer \(n\log n\): still faster than \(n\), so the leader still pulls away, but \(n^2\) overstates it.

The need. 2 a.m. before an exam. Tutoring centers are shut and help is scattered across GroupMe, Discord and Reddit.

Fixed-Capacity Network Good — HuskyHive

Capacity is fixed in the short run. HuskyHive’s servers can serve so many students and no more, so short-run supply is vertical. An airline is the same: no 201st seat on a 200-seat plane.

1. One cluster: 2,000 students. With 2,000 on the app, demand is \(P = 10 - x\). The 2,000th student pays \$8.

2. Capacity comes in lumps. A second cluster, not one more seat: supply jumps to 4,000.

3. A bigger network, a new demand curve. With 4,000 on the app, \(P = 20 - 2x\). The 4,000th student pays \$12.

Supply doubled and the price went up, \$8 → \$12. Capacity is the network here, and the network is part of the product, so supply and demand do not move independently. Both lines still hit zero at 10,000: the last student on campus values the app at \$0.

The Network Demand Curve — HuskyHive

Each thin line is demand for one network size: \(5N\left(1-\tfrac{x}{10}\right)\) with \(N\) fixed. Each one slopes down.

1. 2,000 on the app: the 2,000th student pays \$8.

2. 4,000: \$12. Core Huskies joining make the app worth more: the price rises.

3. 5,000: \$12.50, the top, point c.

4. 6,000: \$12. 8,000: \$8. The students still joining are exam-crammers. Each still adds network value, but they care little, so the price must fall.

5. Join the dots. Set \(N = x = n\): \(P = 5n\left(1-\tfrac{n}{10}\right) = 5n - \tfrac{n^2}{2}\).

The one demand curve in this course that slopes up, then down. No law of demand is broken: every thin line slopes down. Moving along the red curve changes the network itself, so you read across curves.

Market Equilibrium — HuskyHive at \$8

The red hump is HuskyHive’s network demand from before: what the last student to join will pay.

1. The price line. HuskyHive charges \$8 a month.

2. It crosses twice: b at 2,000 students and d at 8,000. At both, the last student values the app at exactly \$8.

3. The peak c is not a crossing. \$12.50 at 5,000 is the most any network size will pay.

4. Nudge it. At 1,000 the last student values it at \$4.50 < \$8: students leave, down to 0. At 3,000, \$10.50 > \$8: more join, up to d. At 9,000, \$4.50: back to d.

b is the tipping point (unstable); d is the stable equilibrium. Below 2,000 a vicious cycle empties the network; above it a virtuous cycle carries it to 8,000. The market settles past the hump, on the falling side.

Finding the Equilibrium — HuskyHive by Algebra

Equilibrium: the last student to join values the app at exactly the price. With \(n\) thousand students and \(P = \$8\): \[5n - \tfrac{n^2}{2} = 8\]

1. Times 2, rearrange: \(\;n^2 - 10n + 16 = 0\)

2. Factor: \((n-2)(n-8) = 0\), so \(n = 2\) (b, 2,000 students) or \(n = 8\) (d, 8,000). For any price: \(n = 5 \pm \sqrt{25 - 2P} = 5 \pm 3\).

4. The ceiling: a root exists only if \(25 - 2P \ge 0\), so \(P \le \$12.50\), the peak c at 5,000. At d: 8,000 × \$8 = \$64,000 a month.

Does the Best Product Win? — Not Always

In a network market a new user weighs quality plus the network. The product that reaches the tipping point first often wins, even if a rival is better.

Value to a new user: \( v = q + a\,N \)  \(q\) = quality, \(N\) = users already on it (millions), \(a = 1\)

A, incumbent
\(q = 10,\ N = 8\)
\(v = 10 + 8 = \mathbf{18}\) ✓

B, better product
\(q = 12,\ N = 2\)
\(v = 12 + 2 = 14\)

The joiner picks A, and A’s lead grows by one more user. B wins only if its quality lead beats \(a(N_A - N_B) = 6\), or if users can use both at once.

QWERTY (1870s)

Still on nearly every keyboard 150 years later. Whether rival layouts are faster is disputed; the installed base of typists keeps it either way.

Path dependence. History and timing, not just quality, pick the winner. Every joiner chooses sensibly, and the market can still lock in the worse product.

Should We Regulate Network Monopolies?

The dilemma. One big network is the efficient size: everyone can reach everyone. But its owner has monopoly power and can shut rivals out.

OPTION 1 · BREAK IT UP

Splitting the network gives each user a smaller network, so the network effect works against the remedy.

2025: the U.S. judge who found Google a search monopolist declined to make it sell Chrome.

OPTION 2 · REGULATE ACCESS

Treat the network as an essential facility and require interconnection: rivals may plug in, as phone carriers must connect each other’s calls.

• Google (Sept 2025): no exclusive default deals; share some search data with rivals.

• WhatsApp (EU, Nov 2025): users can chat with people on rival apps.

App stores: the gates rival apps must pass through.

An airport hub: an essential facility rivals need to reach.

Regulate access, not size. Keep the one big network and make it open. The cost: being first pays less, so firms have less reason to build the next network.

Standards — Why Firms Fight to Set Them

A standard is a shared format — a plug, a tape, a file type — so every product that uses it is on one network. Whoever’s format becomes the standard owns the biggest network.

Why own it?  ① Your installed base becomes everyone’s, so rivals’ customers start using your network.  ② You already make it; rivals must retool.  ③ You collect license fees and steer the next version.

A standards war is a race to the tipping point. The winner’s installed base becomes the industry’s network. A shared or mandated standard makes the whole network bigger, but no single firm owns it.

What Is a Platform? — Pipeline vs Platform

Platform: a business that creates value by matching two or more groups of users who need each other, not by making the good itself.

Pipeline

A carmaker buys steel → builds cars → sells them. Value flows one way, and demand depends on its own price.

Platform: Uber

Owns no cars. It matches riders with drivers: 13.6 billion trips (rides and deliveries) in 2025.

What changes in the economics. Each side’s demand depends on the other side’s size: riders \(D_R(p_R,\, N_D)\), drivers \(S_D(p_D,\, N_R)\). More drivers \(N_D\) means shorter waits, so more riders sign on at the same fare.

So a platform sets two prices, \(p_R\) and \(p_D\), and each one moves participation on both sides.

Two-Sided Platform — Riders, Uber, Drivers

One platform, two groups who need each other. Build it one piece at a time.

Because of the green arrows, the split matters, not just the total: a lower rider fare \(p_R\) also raises what the app is worth to drivers.

The Chicken-and-Egg Problem

No letter wins outright. A ignores that each side’s value depends on the other. B, C or D can each be right: it depends on which side is worth more to the other. In Jan 2016 Uber did B and C at once: fare cuts in about 100 cities and hourly guarantees for drivers.

Why subsidize at all? This is a coordination game with two equilibria: the empty platform and the full one are both self-consistent. With no drivers online, not opening the app is a best response. A subsidy breaks the tie.

Subsidize a side

Pay one side to show up first: hourly guarantees for drivers, cheap first rides for riders.

Borrow a network

Threads launched on Instagram accounts: 100 million sign-ups in five days (Jul 2023).

Why Uber Subsidizes One Side to Win the Other

1 · The dashed line: if people expect a share to join and exactly that share does, expectations come true.

2 · Who joins: expect few drivers, and few riders bother; expect many, and nearly everyone joins. An S-shape.

3 · Three resting points: stuck small (about 4%), critical mass (about 52%, unstable), big and stable (about 88%).

4 · The subsidy’s job: cheap rides and driver guarantees move expectations past critical mass; then the loop carries the platform to the big one.

The poll’s rule: Rochet-Tirole-Weyl price structure. Each side's optimal price reflects (a) its own participation elasticity and (b) the externality its participation creates for the other side. Subsidize the side with the stronger positive cross-side externality.

A \$1 cut on the rider side that attracts 10 riders, each worth \$2 to drivers, loses \$10 in direct revenue but adds \$20 in cross-side value — a net \$10 gain. Keep cutting until marginal direct loss equals marginal cross-side gain.

Peak-Load Pricing — Uber Surge

Peak-load pricing: capacity is fixed in the short run, so charge more when demand is highest. Saturday-night shows, beach rentals in July, Uber when a concert lets out.

Uber surge. At the peak, few drivers are free. Surge raises the fare and the driver’s pay, which pulls more drivers onto the road. That higher pay is why MC is steeper at the peak.

Castillo (2025, Econometrica), with Uber data: against one uniform price level, surge raises total welfare by 2.15% of gross revenue. Riders gain the most; drivers who work long hours lose a little.

Same rule every hour: MR = MC. Off-peak, 30 rides at \$9.80; at the peak, 50 rides at \$22: higher demand and higher MC.

Lock-In — Switching Costs as Market Power

Lock-in: a cost of switching, \(s\), keeps users with a firm even when a rival is a little better or cheaper.

The price it buys. A user stays unless the rival beats the firm by more than \(s\), so the firm can charge up to \(p_{\text{rival}} + s\). Lyft at \$20 and \(s = \$3\): Uber can charge up to \$23. (illustrative)

Bargains first, extraction later. Compete hard for users who are not yet locked in (sign-up promos, cheap first rides), then charge the ones who are.

Uber’s problem: riders and drivers keep Uber and Lyft on one phone, so \(s \approx 0\). Uber builds \(s\): Uber One (46 million members, close to half of gross bookings, end 2025) and Uber Pro driver tiers that reset every three months.

Earned or engineered? The DOJ’s suit against Apple (filed Mar 2024; dismissal denied Jun 2025) says Apple degrades messaging with Android to raise \(s\). Antitrust looks at that line.

Status tiers, paid memberships, rewards networks.

Subsidizing one side isn’t irrational: with cross-side externalities the optimum has unequal prices. Credit cards reward cardholders and charge merchants about 2–3%. Google search is free to users; advertisers pay.

Platform Pricing — Who Pays, and Why

Subsidize

the side whose presence is worth more to the other side. A \$1 rider fare cut loses \$10 in fares and adds \$20 of value for drivers. Cut until the two are equal.

Charge

the side that gains more from the other side and has fewer places to go: drivers pay Uber’s cut, advertisers pay Google.

Price the peak

When drivers are scarce, set MR = MC hour by hour. Surge: total welfare +2.15% of gross revenue, mostly to riders (Castillo 2025).

Lock in, then extract

Switching costs let the subsidy shrink and the cut rise: about 22% of bookings (2019) → 26.9% (2025). Two apps on one phone cap it.

\(p_A = c_A \;-\; \big(\text{value one more } A \text{ brings the other side}\big) \;+\; \text{markup from } A\text{'s own elasticity}\)

Structure, not level: a big enough middle term pushes \(p_A\) below cost, or below zero. Within each side, the menu and bundle tools still apply: UberX vs Black, Uber One.

§12.8 — A Note on Arthur Cecil Pigou (1877–1959)

Pigou was Alfred Marshall's chosen successor at Cambridge, holding the Professor of Political Economy chair from 1908 to 1943. His 1920 The Economics of Welfare did three lasting things: it built the welfare-economics framework for asking when intervention raises social welfare; it introduced the externality; and it classified price discrimination into the first / second / third-degree taxonomy this whole chapter is built on.

That taxonomy is also the bridge to Chapter 17. The Pigouvian tax — a per-unit tax equal to the marginal external cost — internalizes negative externalities and restores efficiency. One economist shapes two parts of this book.

Pigou's three legacies

1. Welfare economics — when does intervention help?

2. The externality — and the Pigouvian tax (Ch 17)

3. The PD taxonomy — this chapter's spine

Chapter Summary

Pigou's taxonomy

First (know each buyer), second (know the distribution), third (observe a group label). Each needs less, extracts less.

Perfect PD

\(Q_{PD} = Q_C\), \(CS = 0\), \(DWL = 0\). Efficient and fully extractive. \(\pi_{PD} = TS_C\).

Third-degree ★

\(L_i = -1/\varepsilon_{D,i}\) per segment. More elastic → lower price. The Ch 11 rule, \(K\) times. But output may not move — and then total surplus falls.

Two-part tariff

Homogeneous: \(p^{\ast} = MC\), \(F^{\ast} = CS(MC)\). Replicates perfect PD. Heterogeneous: exclusion can win.

Bundling

Pure bundling beats separate sale iff \(\text{Cov}(v^X, v^Y) < 0\) (Adams-Yellen). Mixed is never worse — strictly better only if some buyers are lopsided enough to skip the bundle.

Two-sided platforms

Price structure reflects own elasticity + cross-side externality. Subsidize the strong-externality side.

Why five prices? Because one price leaves surplus uncaptured. Each tool here converts consumer surplus into producer surplus. Where the tool raises output (perfect PD, block pricing, the homogeneous two-part tariff) DWL falls. Where it doesn't — third-degree at Starbucks, cups flat at 755 — surplus is merely reshuffled and total welfare can drop. Ask which one you are looking at.
