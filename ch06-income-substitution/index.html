The policy choice: cap the price at last year's level, or let prices rise and hand low-income households cash transfers. Two policies, two different levers — and as Levell, O'Connell & Smith (IFS / Cowles, 2024) document in transaction-level data:

Own-price elasticity of energy demand \(\;\varepsilon_D \in [-0.45,\, -0.31]\) — strong substitution channel. Expenditure elasticity rises from 0.4 (bottom half) to 0.8 (top quintile) — income channel matters too, and unevenly.

A price spike shrinks the whole budget — not just energy

A higher energy price pivots the budget line inward around the unchanged “Other goods” intercept: income is the same, but each unit of energy now costs more.

  • The shaded wedge of bundles is now unaffordable.
  • To keep buying energy, the household must give up other goods — the bill reaches beyond the meter.
  • That squeeze on everything else is the income effect; a cash transfer pushes the whole line back out.

Two policies, two motions: a price cap kills the pivot (substitution); a cash transfer shifts the line out (income).

Two channels, one price spike

Substitution channel

Energy is now expensive relative to everything else. The household

  • turns down the thermostat,
  • weatherizes windows,
  • runs the dishwasher off-peak,
  • defers the next clothes-dryer purchase.

This response would happen even if real income were unchanged.

Income channel

The price spike makes the household effectively poorer (real income falls).

A poorer household cuts spending everywhere — including on necessary energy use that was not substitution-driven at all.

This response would happen even at unchanged prices if income fell by the same dollar amount.

Policy punchline. A price cap mutes substitution (preserves the old price, so the household conserves less). A targeted lump-sum transfer leaves the price signal sharp but neutralizes the income effect for households that need it. The two policies are not equivalent.

The same two channels — in symbols

Substitution effect

\(p_e \uparrow \;\Longrightarrow\; Q_e^{\,d} \downarrow\)

Energy is dearer relative to other goods, so the household substitutes away from it. Always this direction — the compensated move, with real income held fixed.

The guaranteed-negative piece behind the law of demand.

Income effect

A dearer energy bill erodes real income — the same paycheck buys less:

\$5,000 \$4,000 in real purchasing power

even if every price held constant:

\(Q_e^{\,d} \downarrow \quad \text{and} \quad Q_{\text{other}}^{\,d} \downarrow\)

Both fall for normal goods; if energy were inferior the income effect flips (the Giffen case, §6.2).

Total move = substitution + income. The demand response you actually observe stacks the always-negative pivot on top of the real-income loss — and that sum is the Slutsky equation, next.

Two things you buy go up 50% this year: a campus coffee and your rent. Which price rise has the larger income effect — the bigger squeeze on everything else you buy?

A) Coffee — you buy it every day, so it hits constantly
B) Rent — it is a far larger share of your budget
C) The same — both rose 50%
D) Can’t say without knowing your preferences

60 sec to vote

Answer: B — the income channel scales with the budget share

A 50% rise costs you 50% of what you were already spending. On a \$2,000 month with \$800 rent and \$40 of coffee, the rent rise takes \$400 — 20% of the whole budget; the coffee rise takes \$20 — 1%. Same percentage, twentyfold difference in bite.

A confuses frequency with share — a daily purchase can still be a rounding error. C applies the same percentage to very different bases. D is the reflex that everything needs preferences: preferences set the substitution channel, but the income channel’s size is carried by how much you were buying, which is observable.

And the substitution channel ranks the other way. Coffee has close substitutes — make it at home, dining hall, skip it. Rent has almost none inside a semester. So coffee is mostly substitution, rent is mostly income — two goods, two channels, opposite order.

Wednesday the Slutsky equation writes that multiplier down explicitly — the income term carries a factor of how much of the good you were buying.

§6.1  Pure-income changes

10 min · Warm-up: dissect the income channel alone, before we add the price channel

Pure-income thought experiment: Sam's raise

Sam allocates \$60/month between Netflix (\$4) and Spotify (\$6). Cobb-Douglas with \(\alpha = 1/3\). His income doubles to \$120 — neither price changes.

Budget: \(p_1 x_1 + p_2 x_2 = I\) shifts outward in parallel.

  • Both axes rise. Sam spreads new income across both goods (he was buying both already).
  • Slope preserved: \(-p_1/p_2 = -2/3\) unchanged — no relative-price move.
  • Higher indifference curve: \(IC_0 \to IC_1\). Strictly higher utility (monotonicity, §4.1).

Cobb-Douglas

Utility: \(U(x_1, x_2) = x_1^{\alpha}\, x_2^{1-\alpha} = x_1^{1/3}\, x_2^{2/3}\).

Constant-share rule: \(x_1^M = \alpha I/p_1\), \(x_2^M = (1{-}\alpha) I/p_2\).

At \(I=60\): \(A = (5,\, 6.67)\).
At \(I=120\): \(B = (10,\, 13.33)\).

Both quantities double — Cobb-Douglas income elasticity \(=1\) on each good.

Figure 6.12 shows two parallel budget lines and the bundles \(A \to B\) moving northeast.

Sam's response to an income increase

Reading the picture:

  • BC1 → BC2: parallel outward shift — same slope \(-p_1/p_2\), bigger feasible set.
  • A → B: both Netflix and Spotify rise (both normal under Cobb-Douglas).
  • IC0 → IC1: Sam reaches a strictly higher utility.
  • Bundles \(A\) and \(B\) lie on a ray from the origin (slope \((1{-}\alpha) p_1 / (\alpha\, p_2) = 4/3\)) — a Cobb-Douglas signature we return to shortly.

Empirical claim. Income doubled, both quantities doubled. Both income elasticities \(=1\) — the Cobb-Douglas signature.

When one good is inferior (Carlos's case)

Contrast with Sam:

Income elasticity: necessity, luxury, inferior

\[\varepsilon_I \;=\; \frac{\Delta x / x}{\Delta I / I}\;=\;\frac{\partial x}{\partial I}\cdot\frac{I}{x}.\]

Sign of \(\varepsilon_I\) sets normal vs. inferior; magnitude inside the normal range further splits necessity vs. luxury.

Necessity

\(0 < \varepsilon_I < 1\)

Quantity rises with income, slower than income. Budget share falls. Examples: toothpaste, salt, food-at-home, electricity.

Luxury

\(\varepsilon_I > 1\)

Quantity rises faster than income. Budget share rises. Examples: jewelry, first-class tickets, vacation homes, restaurant meals.

Inferior

\(\varepsilon_I < 0\)

Quantity falls as income rises. Trade up to a preferred alternative. Examples: store-brand canned soup, bus rides, used cars, ramen.

Adding-up constraint. Not every good can be inferior simultaneously. If Sam cuts bus rides when his income rises, the saved dollars go somewhere. At least one good in the bundle must be normal.

Normal vs. inferior can flip with income level. A used car is normal at low income (any car beats the bus) and inferior at high income (new cars supplant used ones).

Income Expansion Path and Engel curve

Income Expansion Path (IEP)

Trace the consumer's optimum bundle from \(I = 0\) (the origin) through every increasing income level. Connect the tangencies.

  • Two normal goods: IEP slopes upward — every income increase moves the bundle northeast.
  • Normal + eventually-inferior: IEP bends backward on the inferior axis past some income threshold.

Lives in the same \((x_1, x_2)\) plane as the indifference curves.

Engel curve (Engel, 1857)

Replot: income on the vertical axis, quantity of one good on the horizontal. One point per IEP point.

  • Positive slope ⇒ normal good at that income.
  • Negative slope ⇒ inferior at that income.
  • Backward bend ⇒ normal-to-inferior transition.

Engel curve = empirical workhorse. Household surveys (BLS CEX, USDA ERS) record income + spending; plotting the data gives the Engel curve directly. The slope at any point estimates \(\varepsilon_I\) at that income range. T&D 6.1 below uses BLS CEX 2024 food-share data to verify Engel's law (food share falls with income).

Drag income low → high:

Application — Aguiar & Bils on restaurants as luxury

Aguiar & Bils (2015, AER, DOI 10.1257/aer.20120599) estimated Engel curves on CEX + PSID data for a wide range of consumer goods. Two headline findings:

Food at home

\(\varepsilon_I \approx 0.4\)

Necessity. +10% income ⇒ +4% spend.

Restaurant meals

\(\varepsilon_I \approx 1.3\)

Luxury. +10% income ⇒ +13% spend.

Why it matters

3-to-1 luxury-vs-necessity gap. As US incomes have risen over the past 50 years, restaurant spending has grown about three times faster than grocery spending.

The long-run US restaurant boom — chains, delivery apps, ghost kitchens — is partly the mechanical consequence of an \(\varepsilon_I = 1.3\) good in a rising-income economy.

Application 6.1 below (the gas-elasticity story) is the price-channel analog. Aguiar & Bils give the income-channel benchmark — two empirical anchors for the chapter's two channels.

§6.2  How price changes affect consumption choices

Substitution and income effects on an indifference map

Deriving a demand curve

Just as income shifts the bundle (§6.1), changes in relative prices — holding income constant — also reshape consumer choices. We now build Sam's individual demand curve directly from his utility model.

The recipe

  • A demand curve maps quantity demanded to price, holding income and other prices fixed.
  • To trace it, change one price on the indifference-map / budget-constraint figure and read off the new optimum.
  • Each price → one optimum → one \((Q, P)\) point on the demand curve.
  • Connect the points to get the consumer's demand curve for that good.

The observed price represents the consumer's maximum willingness to pay for the last unit consumed — the inverse-demand interpretation we use in welfare analysis (Ch 2 CS, Ch 6.7 CV).

Shifts in the demand curve

Movement along the demand curve answers “more Netflix at lower price.” A shift of the whole curve answers a different question: at the same price, has the consumer's demand changed?

What shifts the curve

  • Preferences change (taste shock; new info; ad campaign).
  • Income changes (the §6.1 channel).
  • Prices of other goods change (cross-price effects).

Sam's preference shock

Suppose Sam starts listening to more new music (podcasts, lossless audio) and values Netflix less, relatively speaking. His Cobb-Douglas weight \(\alpha\) on Netflix drops from \(1/3\) to \(1/5\).

At every Netflix price, Sam wants fewer Netflix subs — the whole demand curve shifts inward.

Mechanism: with lower \(\alpha\), Sam's indifference curves flatten in the Netflix direction — tangency on the same budget line now sits further left.

Simple rules — substitution vs. income effects

Substitution effects

comparing two bundles on the same indifference curve

  • Direction is unambiguous — a theorem, not a finding.
  • Relative price falls ⇒ the consumer wants more of it.
  • Relative price rises ⇒ the consumer wants less.
  • Why no ambiguity? Convex indifference curves. Sliding along a fixed \(U\) always moves you toward whatever got cheaper.

Income effects

comparing bundles on two different indifference curves

  • Direction is ambiguous — it depends on the good.
  • Normal good: a fall in either price ⇒ wants more. Any price drop raises real income.
  • Inferior good: that same price drop ⇒ wants less. The income term flips.
  • A price rise mirrors both: normal ⇒ less, inferior ⇒ more.

What we actually observe is the total effect \(=\) unambiguous substitution \(+\) ambiguous income. The ambiguity in demand comes from one of the two channels, never both.

Cross-price effects shift the demand curve

Classify by the sign of the cross-price effect:

Substitutes  \(\partial x_i/\partial p_j > 0\)

Pepsi and Coke

Complements  \(\partial x_i/\partial p_j < 0\)

ice cream and hot fudge

Rightward: \(D_1 \to D_2\)

More of X at every price. A substitute’s price rises, or a complement’s price falls.

Leftward: \(D_1 \to D_3\)

Less of X at every price. A substitute’s price falls, or a complement’s price rises.

Another good’s price shifts X's demand curve. Only X’s own price moves you along it.

Application — movies: theaters vs. home streaming

The pandemic as a natural experiment

COVID-19 effectively raised the “price” (risk + closures) of going to a theater. So which way did home streaming move?

  • If theaters and streaming were complements \(\Rightarrow\) streaming should have declined too.
  • If they were substitutes \(\Rightarrow\) streaming should have surged.

The verdict — they are substitutes

  • Theater box office: U.S. revenue collapsed from \(\$11.3\,\text{B}\) in 2019 to \(\$2.1\,\text{B}\) in 2020.
  • Home streaming: Netflix subscriptions grew +45%; Disney+ reached 40 M U.S. subscribers.

Not new — the substitution accelerated a historical pattern: in 1946 the average American went to the movies 28 times/year; a decade later, with TVs widespread, that number was cut in half. COVID just compressed decades of substitution into one year.

At each price, add the quantities. Market demand is the horizontal sum of individual demands. Video games:

§6.3  The Slutsky equation

First: the cheapest way to stay equally happy · then the headline identity and two walkthroughs

Two questions, one bundle

The Ch 5 question

Budget given — how happy can you get?

\(\max\, U(x)\ \text{ s.t. }\ p\cdot x \le I\)

Answer: Marshallian demand \(x_i(p, I)\). What we actually observe.

The new question

Happiness given — what is the cheapest way there?

\(\min\, p\cdot x\ \text{ s.t. }\ U(x) \ge \bar u\)

Answer: Hicksian demand \(h_i(p, \bar u)\). Its minimum cost is the expenditure function \(e(p, \bar u)\).

You have already found one: \(A'\) in the Chipotle poll was a Hicksian bundle — happiness held at \(U_1\), new prices.

Duality: same tangency, two routes

\(h_i\big(p,\, v(p, I)\big) \;=\; x_i(p, I) \qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad x_i\big(p,\, e(p, \bar u)\big) \;=\; h_i(p, \bar u)\)

At the utility you actually reach, the two questions pick the same bundle. Differentiate this in \(p_j\) and you get the Slutsky equation.

The expenditure function \(e(p, \bar u)\)

\[e(p, \bar u) \;=\; \min_{x} \{\, p \cdot x \,:\, U(x) \geq \bar u \,\} \;=\; p_1 h_1 + p_2 h_2\]

The cheapest bill that still reaches happiness \(\bar u\) at prices \(p\).

Four properties to know:

1. Rises with any price

Non-decreasing in each \(p_i\): higher prices, weakly higher bill.

2. HOD 1 in prices

\(e(\alpha p, \bar u) = \alpha\, e(p, \bar u)\). Double every price and the bill doubles.

3. Concave in prices

When one price rises you switch away from that good, so the bill rises by less than if you kept buying the same bundle.

Not convex — the most common mix-up

4. Rises with \(\bar u\)

A higher happiness floor costs more.

The Slutsky equation

For any pair of goods \(i, j\), any consumer with smooth preferences, evaluated at the bundle that achieves utility \(\bar u = v(p, I)\):

\[\boxed{\;\frac{\partial x_i^M(p, I)}{\partial p_j} \;=\; \underbrace{\frac{\partial h_i(p, \bar u)}{\partial p_j}}_{\text{substitution effect}} \;-\; \underbrace{x_j^M(p, I) \cdot \frac{\partial x_i^M(p, I)}{\partial I}}_{\text{income effect}}.\;}\]

LHS: what the data show

How Marshallian (actual) demand responds to a price change. Both channels mixed.

RHS: the decomposition

Hicksian response (pure substitution) minus the dollar value of \(x_j\) times income response.

The income term is multiplied by \(x_j\) because a unit rise in \(p_j\) costs the consumer roughly \(x_j\) extra dollars to keep buying the original bundle — that's the “real income loss” we have to subtract back out.

CalculusCorner 6.2 — derivation in three lines

Duality identity (start)

\(x_i^M\!\big(p,\, e(p, \bar u)\big) \;=\; h_i(p, \bar u).\)

Step 1. Differentiate both sides w.r.t. \(p_j\) (chain rule on LHS):

\(\dfrac{\partial x_i^M}{\partial p_j} + \dfrac{\partial x_i^M}{\partial I} \!\cdot\! \dfrac{\partial e(p, \bar u)}{\partial p_j} = \dfrac{\partial h_i}{\partial p_j}.\)

Step 2. Apply Shephard's lemma — the derivative of the expenditure function in \(p_j\) is the Hicksian quantity \(h_j\); you saw its geometry on the concavity figure, where the tangent's slope was \(x_1^0\). It's an envelope-theorem result — proof in §6.5; take it on faith today:

\(\dfrac{\partial x_i^M}{\partial p_j} + \dfrac{\partial x_i^M}{\partial I} \!\cdot\! x_j^M = \dfrac{\partial h_i}{\partial p_j}.\)

Step 3. Rearrange to land the Slutsky equation:

\(\boxed{\;\dfrac{\partial x_i^M}{\partial p_j} \;=\; \dfrac{\partial h_i}{\partial p_j} \;-\; x_j^M \!\cdot\! \dfrac{\partial x_i^M}{\partial I}.\;}\)

The Slutsky equation is the picture you already drew

\(\dfrac{\partial x_1}{\partial p_1} \;=\; \dfrac{\partial h_1}{\partial p_1} \;-\; x_1\,\dfrac{\partial x_1}{\partial I}\)

Each term is one leg of the decomposition you built for Sam:

Total  \(\dfrac{\partial x_1}{\partial p_1}\)

A → B · what the data show

Sam: good 1 +2  ·  good 2 +1

Substitution  \(\dfrac{\partial h_1}{\partial p_1}\)

A → A′ · along U₁

Sam: good 1 +1.24  ·  good 2 −1.76

Income  \(-\,x_1\dfrac{\partial x_1}{\partial I}\)

A′ → B · purchasing power

Sam: good 1 +0.76  ·  good 2 +2.76

The equation is for a small price change: multiply each term by the change in \(p_1\) and you get one leg. Here the price fell, so the income leg adds; when a price rises, it subtracts.

Walkthrough 6.1 — Sam’s Netflix price hike

The problem

Sam has \$60 a month. \(U = x_1^{1/3} x_2^{2/3}\), with \(x_1\) = Netflix, \(x_2\) = Spotify at \$6.

Netflix goes from \$4 to \$6. How much of his Netflix cut is substitution, and how much is income?

What we can see — Ch 5 demand, \(x_1 = \tfrac{1}{3}\cdot 60 / p_1 = 20/p_1\)

A at \$4: \(x_1 = 20/4 = 5\),  \(x_2 = 40/6 = 6.67\)

…and after the price rise

B at \$6: \(x_1 = 20/6 = 3.33\),  \(x_2 = 6.67\)

Total: 3.33 − 5 = −1.67 Netflix subscriptions.

What we can’t see: A′ — the bundle Sam would pick at the new prices if he were exactly as happy as at A. Find it, and the split is subtraction.

Walkthrough 6.1 — find A′, then subtract

A′ obeys the same two rules as the slider:

Rule 1 · new prices (tangency)

\(\text{MRS} = \dfrac{x_2}{2x_1} = \dfrac{p_1^\prime}{p_2} = \dfrac{6}{6}\)  ⇒  \(x_2 = 2x_1\)

Rule 2 · same happiness as A

\(\bar u = 5^{1/3}(6.67)^{2/3} \approx 6.06\),  so  \(x_1^{1/3}(2x_1)^{2/3} = 6.06\)

⇒ \(x_1 = 6.06 / 2^{2/3} \approx 3.82\),  A′ = (3.82, 7.63)

Substitution  \(3.82 - 5 = \mathbf{-1.18}\)

A → A′

Income  \(3.33 - 3.82 = \mathbf{-0.48}\)

A′ → B

Total −1.67 = −1.18 + −0.48 ✓ Both negative: Netflix is a normal good, and substitution is the bigger piece (71%).

Self-check: an own-price substitution effect is always ≤ 0. If yours is positive, the algebra slipped.

Walkthrough 6.2 — Ana’s soda doubles

The problem

Ana has \$20 a week. \(U = 6\sqrt{x_1} + x_2\): soda \(x_1\), and \(x_2\) = dollars on everything else.

Soda goes from \$1 to \$2 a can. Split her soda cut. Then: how much extra cash would make her exactly as happy as before? That cash is the compensating variation (CV).

What we can see — her demand, from the tangency

\(MU_1 = 6\cdot\tfrac{1}{2\sqrt{x_1}} = \tfrac{3}{\sqrt{x_1}}\),  \(MU_2 = 1\)  ⇒  \(\text{MRS} = \tfrac{3}{\sqrt{x_1}}\)

Set \(\text{MRS} = p_1/p_2 = p_1\):  \(\sqrt{x_1} = 3/p_1\)  ⇒  \(x_1 = 9/p_1^2\)

A at \$1: \(x_1 = 9\), \(x_2 = 20 - 9 = 11\)

…and after the price doubles

B at \$2: \(x_1 = 2.25\), \(x_2 = 15.5\)

Total: 2.25 − 9 = −6.75 cans.

Look at \(x_1 = 9/p_1^2\): there is no \(I\) in it. Income cannot move Ana’s soda. Keep that in mind for the split.

Walkthrough 6.2 — find A′, then the make-whole cash

Same two rules as Sam:

Rule 1 · new prices (tangency)

\(3/\sqrt{x_1} = p_1^\prime = 2\)  ⇒  \(x_1 = 2.25\)

Rule 2 · same happiness as A

\(6\sqrt{2.25} + x_2 = 29\)

⇒ \(x_2 = 20\),  A′ = (2.25, 20)

Substitution  \(2.25 - 9 = \mathbf{-6.75}\)

A → A′

Income  \(2.25 - 2.25 = \mathbf{0}\) — straight down

A′ → B

CV  the extra cash that keeps Ana on \(U_0\) at the new price: \(\text{CV} = e(p^\prime, \bar u_0) - I\)

A′ costs \(2(2.25) + 20 = \$24.50\), so CV = \$24.50 − \$20 = \$4.50

That is the gap from A′ down to B. Check: area under demand, \(\int_1^2 9/p^2\,dp = \$4.50\) ✓

Poll: what would it take?

 No smartphone for one week, starting tonight. Not locked in a drawer — gone. Your laptop stays.

Each number is a CV: the cash that puts you back on your old indifference curve after something bad happens — Rule 2 from Ana’s soda, applied to your own week. The median is the class’s CV.

Add the CVs and you have the cost of a “no phones for a week” policy.

Regulators ask the same question when a merger takes a firm out of a market: how much would every buyer need to be paid, summed?

CV in practice — antitrust & merger analysis

Regulators and economists use CV to put a dollar number on consumer harm from a price increase — the core empirical input to merger and antitrust decisions.

The DOJ/FTC merger playbook

  1. Estimate the post-merger price increase (UPP / GUPPI / simulation).
  2. Multiply by quantity to get aggregate CV — the dollar value of consumer harm.
  3. Compare against merger-specific efficiencies (cost savings passed to consumers).
  4. Block / challenge / clear based on net welfare effect.

Concrete cases — CV at work

  • T-Mobile / Sprint (2020): the review turned on predicted price increases — the CV logic; cleared with divestiture (Dish enters as 4th carrier).
  • Hospital merger studies (Cooper et al. 2019): CV from local hospital mergers averages 1–3% of healthcare spending in concentrated markets.
  • Ticketmaster / Live Nation (2010): CV estimates for higher concert prices were key to the conduct-remedy decree.

Ana's shortcut — but only approximately. Wireless plans, hospital visits and concert tickets are large dollar shares of a household budget, so the income effect isn't zero the way it was for Ana's soda — strictly, \(CV \ne\) consumer surplus here. But for small price changes (say, a 5% increase from a merger) that income effect is tiny, so the Ch 2 area under demand approximates CV well enough for legal decisions. Hausman (1981) makes the approximation rigorous.

CV is what the Slutsky machinery is for in policy — not just a homework variable.

§6.4  Sign classification: normal, inferior, Giffen

Read the sign off one equation · then Carlos’s soup

Normal, inferior, Giffen — one equation decides

\[\boxed{\;\frac{\partial x_i^M(p, I)}{\partial p_j} \;=\; \underbrace{\frac{\partial h_i(p, \bar u)}{\partial p_j}}_{\text{substitution effect}} \;-\; \underbrace{x_j^M(p, I) \cdot \frac{\partial x_i^M(p, I)}{\partial I}}_{\text{income effect}}.\;}\]

Substitution is always ≤ 0. The income term’s sign decides the case:

Normal · \(\partial x_i^M/\partial I > 0\)

Income adds to substitution. Marshallian is flatter than Hicksian — more price-responsive. Coffee, phones, streaming.

Inferior · \(\partial x_i^M/\partial I < 0\)

Income pushes back, but substitution wins. Marshallian is steeper — still slopes down. Store-brand soup.

Giffen · inferior, and income wins

Marshallian slopes up. Rare: very poor households, a staple that is a big budget share (Hunan rice).

Walkthrough 6.3 — Carlos’s store-brand soup

The problem

Carlos’s household spends \$5,000 a month. Store-brand soup goes from \$4 to \$6 a can (pantry items stay at \$8).

Survey data give his demand: \(x_1 = 30 - 2p_1 + 0.5p_2 - 0.001\,I\). No utility function — split the change anyway.

Read two slopes off the demand

Own price: \(\partial x_1/\partial p_1 = -2\) cans per \$

Income: \(\partial x_1/\partial I = -0.001\) — negative, so soup is inferior

What we can see

A at \$4: \(x_1 = 30 - 8 + 4 - 5 = 21\) cans

B at \$6: \(x_1 = 17\) cans.  Total: −4

Inferior means the two legs fight. The data show only the total. Next: how much is substitution, how much is income?

Walkthrough 6.3 — the legs fight, substitution wins

Step 1 · solve Slutsky for the piece we can’t see

\(\dfrac{{\partial h_1}}{{\partial p_1}} = \dfrac{{\partial x_1}}{{\partial p_1}} + x_1\dfrac{{\partial x_1}}{{\partial I}}\)

\(= -2 + 21(-0.001) = \mathbf{-2.021}\) cans per \$

Step 2 · demand is linear, so multiply each slope by the \$2 rise:

Substitution  \(-2.021 \times 2 = \mathbf{-4.042}\)

Income  \(-21 \times (-0.001) \times 2 = \mathbf{+0.042}\)

pushes back

Total −4 = −4.042 + 0.042 ✓ Inferior, but not Giffen: substitution wins.

Could it ever be Giffen? Only if \(0.001\,x_1 > 2.021\), i.e. \(x_1 > 2{,}021\) cans a month. No.

When income wins: Giffen goods

What it is

The price rises and you buy more. The demand curve slopes up.

How it can happen

If the good is most of your budget, a price rise makes you much poorer. If it is inferior, poorer means you want more of it — and that pull beats substitution.

Three ingredients — all needed

① inferior  ② a huge share of the budget  ③ near subsistence: it is the cheap way to get enough calories

Substitution never flips: the Hicksian line still slopes down. Only the income pull is big enough to tip the Marshallian.

Don’t confuse it with a Veblen good

A designer bag can sell more when its price rises — because the high price is part of what you are buying: status. That is a different story. Tastes depend on the price, so it is not income beating substitution, and it is the rich who do it, not the poor.

Giffen goods: the myth and the evidence

The myth · Irish potatoes, 1840s

For a century, textbooks said poor Irish families bought more potatoes as the famine drove prices up.

Why it fails

The blight cut supply, and prices soared. Reanalyses of the data (Dwyer & Lindsay 1984; Rosen 1999) find an ordinary, downward-sloping demand — no Giffen behaviour.

The evidence · Hunan rice, 2006

Jensen & Miller (AER 2008) gave randomly chosen poor households vouchers that cut the price of rice.

What happened

Many households bought less rice: the cheaper staple freed money for meat and vegetables. The very poorest did not — they still needed every calorie rice gave them.

Giffen lives in a narrow band: poor enough that a staple is most of the budget, but not so poor that you are at the calorie floor. And randomisation is what makes it believable — the potato story never had that.

Walkthrough 6.4 — the Chen family and rice

The problem

The Chens, rural Hunan, have \$100 a month. A rice voucher — like Jensen & Miller’s — cuts rice from \$3 to \$2 a kg. Meat and vegetables cost \$4 a serving.

An illustrative demand: \(x_1 = 31.5 + 0.5\,p_1 - 0.03\,I\).

Read two slopes off the demand

Own price: \(\partial x_1/\partial p_1 = \mathbf{+0.5}\) — positive!

Income: \(\partial x_1/\partial I = -0.03\) — strongly inferior

What we can see

A at \$3: \(x_1 = 31.5 + 1.5 - 3 = 30\) kg — \$90, 90% of the budget

B at \$2: \(x_1 = 29.5\) kg (\$59; meat jumps from 2.5 servings to 10). Total: −0.5

Rice got cheaper and they bought less — demand slopes up. But substitution always pushes toward the cheaper good — so where does −0.5 come from?

Walkthrough 6.4 — the legs fight, income wins

Step 1 · the substitution slope (same move as Carlos)

\(\dfrac{{\partial h_1}}{{\partial p_1}} = \dfrac{{\partial x_1}}{{\partial p_1}} + x_1\dfrac{{\partial x_1}}{{\partial I}}\)

\(= 0.5 + 30(-0.03) = \mathbf{-0.4}\) — negative ✓, theory holds

Step 2 · multiply each slope by the price change, −\$1:

Substitution  \(-0.4 \times (-1) = \mathbf{+0.4}\)

Income  \(-30 \times (-0.03) \times (-1) = \mathbf{-0.9}\)

wins

Total −0.5 = +0.4 − 0.9 ✓ Hicksian slopes down, Marshallian slopes up — both at once.

Threshold check: Giffen needs \(x_1 > 0.4/0.03 \approx 13\) kg. The Chens eat 30.

HeadsUp 6.2 — what “compensated” means

“Compensated demand” means utility is held constant, not income. Students consistently swap these.

Hicksian (compensated)

Fix the utility floor \(\bar u\). As prices change, the income required to reach \(\bar u\) changes — that's exactly \(e(p, \bar u)\). The consumer is “compensated” for the price change with whatever income keeps them on the same indifference curve.

NOT “income-held-constant”

Marshallian demand is income-held-constant: fix \(I\), let utility float. That's the consumer's actual behavior. The Hicksian thought experiment is the opposite: fix utility, let income float.

Mnemonic. Hicksian = “Hold utility”. Marshallian = “Money fixed”. The two coincide at one point (the original \((p, I)\)) and diverge as relative prices change.

Key takeaways

  1. Every price change has two legs. Substitution: new prices, same happiness (slide BC₂ to the old curve, find \(A^\prime\)). Income: the rest, from \(A^\prime\) to \(B\).
  2. The Slutsky equation says the same thing in symbols: \(\partial x_i/\partial p_j = \partial h_i/\partial p_j - x_j\,\partial x_i/\partial I\). The substitution term is always ≤ 0.
  3. The income term’s sign sorts goods. Normal: it adds. Inferior: it pushes back and loses (Carlos). Giffen: it wins — rare, needs a staple that is most of a poor budget (the Chens, Hunan rice).
  4. Compensating variation is the cash that restores the old happiness. With quasilinear tastes it equals the area under demand (Ana). Summed across people, it prices a policy or a merger.
  5. Cross-price effects shift demand: substitutes have a positive cross-price effect, complements a negative one. Market demand is the horizontal sum of individual demands.
